Every winter is different, so trying to find the perfect allegory from a prior year won't get you very far. What can hold more weight are the general relationships: when the PNA, ENSO and the MJO have been in a given state, how did Washington's SNOTEL stations do on average? We compare the current state of the oceanic and atmospheric teleconnections to those from the past 35 cold-seasons to see what the next two weeks might bring, or say that the signal is too weak to be meaningful. You can also type in numbers of your own to see what could happen.

Storm patterns, and what the Euro has coming

On the left: six upper-level patterns that Washington storm days fall into, found by clustering the position and depth of the lowest 500 hPa height anomaly (west of Anchorage's longitude is ignored) on 2,111 storm days from November 15 to April 30, 1992 to 2026 (ERA5). A storm day is one when the Cascades SNOTEL sites averaged at least a quarter inch of new water (a tenth in April). On the right: the ECMWF forecast for the next several days, plotted the same way over the same region, so you can eyeball which pattern it looks like. Shading is how unusual the 500 hPa height or the 850 hPa temperature is for the date; the star is the Cascades.

Euro forecast
  • Patterns 0 and 1 (a deep low west or northwest of the Cascades, with moisture reaching the coast) both favor Hurricane Ridge; pattern 0 also favors Mt. Baker, and pattern 1 favors Washington Pass, Blewett, Stevens and Snoqualmie.
  • Pattern 4 (a cold, moderate low to the north at the Cascades' longitude) is the one that favors Paradise and White Pass.
  • Pattern 3 (the low well to the south, over California) is the one that shortchanges the northern zones.
  • The limits: six groups are a simplification of a continuum of patterns. Refitting on resampled winters reproduces the grouping only moderately (adjusted Rand index of about 0.7, where 1 is identical), and some starred cells will be chance. The Euro maps use ECMWF's open-data forecasts: the deterministic run is a single forecast, and the ensemble view is the mean of 50 members (an ensemble mean smooths out features that members disagree on, so it looks weaker than any one forecast will). Anomalies come from a smooth ERA5 day-of-year average, so the first days of November and the end of April are the least reliable.

Check the outlook yourself

The predictor above uses the observed MJO and the GEFS PNA forecast. The MJO forecasts below are not published as data we can read, so for the second week it leaves the MJO out until the observed values arrive; if you read a phase from these figures, choose “Your own values” above and enter it. The MJO matters about eight days after the phase it is in. The SEAS5 PNA chart is a monthly outlook, so it is only a tilt.

How it works, and what it cannot do

  • What is read automatically (updated daily): the observed daily PNA and the GEFS ensemble forecast of it (31 members, 15 days; the 5-day mean of the ensemble mean is used, and the spread among members widens the range), the observed MJO from NOAA PSL’s real-time ROMI index (rotated to match the phase numbering the relationships were built with; 100% of days within one phase of the OMI, 69% exactly), and the latest monthly ONI and PDO.
  • The data behind the relationships: 36 Washington SNOTEL stations with records back to 1990 or earlier, every day from November through May, 1992 to 2026. For each day we measured temperature compared with normal, new snow (daily gain in snow water equivalent), rain (precipitation that did not add to the snowpack) and big snow days, then fit all the indices at once so that one does not take credit for another. The analysis is on the Teleconnections page.
  • The rule: the prediction is the sum of the effects of the three indices, added to what the MJO phase has meant eight days later. A pattern is only named (cooler, warmer, snowier, less snow, rainier) when its range of uncertainty does not include zero; otherwise it is reported as no clear signal.
  • PDO: it is in the fit, but once ENSO and the PNA are known it adds no independent information we can detect. Leave it where it is.
  • The limits: these are averages over many winters, not a forecast of any one storm. Effects add up in the model but may not in the atmosphere. The uncertainty shown is approximate (the ranges of the parts are combined as if independent), and with only 35 winters, ENSO and the PDO have few independent samples. A no clear signal answer is common.
