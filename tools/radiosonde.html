Radiosonde Skew-T

Observed soundings for Quillayute, Salem, Spokane, and Port Hardy, rendered client-side with MetPy from IEM's RAOB data feed. Useful for eyeballing the true snow level (not just the freezing level), the dendritic growth zone (DGZ), and convective potential. Nothing here is forecast data -- it's what the last balloon actually measured. Hover the chart to read temperature, dew point, and parcel values at any level, in metric or imperial units.

Sounding Diagnostics

Snow Level i
—

What is this?

The altitude where a falling ensemble of small/medium/large snowflakes has melted at least 95% of its mass, from a simplified Matsuo & Sasyo (1981) melting-rate model run on this sounding, using the sub-cloud air exactly as observed. This is usually meaningfully lower than the freezing level or wet-bulb-zero height below, since snow keeps melting as it falls through above-freezing air. If this sounding was launched during dry/non-precipitating conditions, see "Snow Level (Saturated Column)" for why this number can look surprisingly low.

Snow Level (Saturated Column) i
—

What is this?

The same snow-level calculation, but assuming the sub-cloud air is fully saturated (dewpoint = temperature) rather than using this sounding's actual, possibly-drier dewpoint. This approximates what happens once precipitation has been falling long enough to saturate that layer through evaporative cooling. If it's noticeably higher than the plain Snow Level above, this sounding was likely dry/non-precipitating at launch -- the real snow level right now depends on whether (and how long) it's actually been snowing/raining, so treat these two numbers as a plausible range rather than picking one.

Freezing Level i
—

What is this?

The height above ground where the observed (dry-bulb) air temperature first drops to 0°C. This is the classic "freezing level," but by itself it doesn't tell you where snow actually reaches the ground -- see Snow Level.

Wet-Bulb Zero i
—

What is this?

The height where the wet-bulb temperature (which accounts for evaporative cooling in unsaturated air) drops to 0°C. This is always at or below the dry-bulb freezing level, and marks where a falling snowflake actually starts melting.

Melting Distance i
—

What is this?

How far (vertically) a medium-size (3 mm) snowflake falls below the wet-bulb-zero height before it's fully melted, per this model. A short distance means a sharp rain/snow transition; a long distance means a mushy, gradual one.

Thermal-wind signal i
—

What is this?

Whether the wind veers (turns clockwise with height, implying warm advection) or backs (turns counterclockwise, implying cold advection) between the surface and roughly 1-2.5 km up, estimated from the thermal-wind relationship.

Dendritic growth zone i
—

What is this?

The depth (in km AGL) of the layer between -12°C and -18°C, where stellar dendrite snowflakes grow most efficiently. Shaded blue on the skew-T above.

Surface Precipitation Type

Most Likely i
—

What is this?

A simplified version of the Modified Bourgouin technique (Birk et al. 2021, Weather and Forecasting, building on Bourgouin 2000), which classifies rain/snow/sleet/freezing rain from how much melting energy exists aloft versus how much refreezing energy exists near the surface. Unlike the Snow Level model above, this handles "warm nose" soundings -- a melting layer aloft over sub-freezing surface air -- which is exactly the sleet/freezing-rain setup that model isn't designed for. Percentages are independent (not required to sum to 100%) since mixed precipitation is physically possible.

Convection

MLCAPE i
—

What is this?

Mixed-Layer CAPE: convective available potential energy for a parcel representing the average of roughly the lowest 100 hPa. More conservative and generally preferred by severe-weather forecasters, since it reflects what the atmosphere is actually doing as a whole rather than a single hot spot.

MUCAPE · source height i
—

What is this?

Most-Unstable CAPE: the maximum energy available to a parcel lifted from the single most unstable level in the sounding. Tells you the absolute maximum energy potential available to a storm, even if that level isn't at the surface (e.g. the ground is cold but a warm, moist layer sits just above it).
